Domain

What the literature already knows, and what a farm still cannot do.

The study sits in precision livestock farming. The contribution is one farmer-facing system that joins image diagnosis, behaviour, manual weight measurement, feeding, nutrition, reproduction, cattle identity, and a local milk-price forecast.

01

Literature survey

Precision livestock farming treats each animal as the unit of management. Sensors, images, and models turn routine signals into a decision while there is still time to act. Berckmans described this shift as continuous, individual monitoring rather than a herd average checked by eye.

Seeing disease in an image

Skin and udder disease is a computer-vision problem when the sign is visible: nodules of lumpy skin disease, lesions of dermatophilosis or ringworm, the vesicles of foot-and-mouth disease, lice of pediculosis, or the swollen quarter of mastitis. Convolutional networks, especially densely connected nets such as DenseNet, reuse features across layers and train well when veterinary images are scarce. The YOLO family, including YOLOv8, is used when the same image must be screened quickly. Studies usually report one architecture. A farm photo is messy — glare, mud, partial bodies — so a second model is a practical check, not a duplicate.

Behaviour before the lesion

Eating time, lying time, steps, and rumination move before a farmer sees a clinical sign. Precision-livestock work treats those shifts as an early-warning channel. Video detectors can label eating, standing, lying, and walking without a collar on every cow. This project keeps both channels: a still image for the visible disease, and behaviour for the animal that is not yet obviously ill.

Feed, weight, and nutrients

Ration rules have long used body weight, milk yield, breed, and stage of lactation. On this project, body weight is a manual weight measurement. The farmer enters a reading from a scale or a tape. That figure, with breed, age, milk yield, and activity, drives the daily feed suggestion. A separate recommender then sets dry-matter intake and minerals from health and production inputs, so a diseased cow is not offered the ration of a healthy one.

Birth, incubation, and the milk cheque

Calving prediction in the literature uses temperature, restlessness, and late-gestation measures. Hatchability studies use incubation temperature, humidity, egg weight, turning, and day of set. Milk-price work is a separate time-series problem: quality (fat and solids-not-fat), season, and volume move the rupee value of a litre. Farmers meet all three on the same week. The papers rarely do.

Knowing which cow

Individual identity is the join key. Coat and body detection with an object detector lets a phone attach a diagnosis, a feed plan, or a birth estimate to a record instead of to “a cow in the lane”.

Anchors used while reading: Berckmans on precision livestock farming; Huang et al. on DenseNet; the YOLO line of detectors through Ultralytics YOLOv8; Friedman on gradient boosting; dairy nutrient-requirement tables for ration logic. Figures quoted on this site are from this project’s own trained models, not from those papers.

02

Research gap

Tools are separate

A disease classifier, a feed sheet, a hatch calculator, and a price chart live in different papers and different apps. The farmer is the integration layer.

Diagnosis arrives late

Visual checks happen when milk has already fallen or a lesion is obvious. Behaviour is watched, but not scored against that cow’s baseline.

One model, one confidence

Field images punish a single network. Few farm systems compare two vision models and keep the stronger call before severity and treatment are estimated.

Advice ignores the Sri Lankan week

Market models are rarely tied to disease stage and feed quality, and they are rarely expressed as next month’s income in rupees for the litres that farm actually produced.

03

Research problem

Traditional cattle keeping still depends on memory and a late visual check. Disease is named after it has spread or after yield has dropped. Feed is a habit rather than a function of weight, milk, and health. Calving and hatching are prepared for at the last moment. The milk price is discovered when the buyer announces it.

The problem addressed here is the absence of one usable decision path that can, from a phone, identify the animal, read a photograph or video, combine simple clinical numbers, and return a disease call with severity and treatment, a feed and nutrition suggestion, a birth or hatch estimate, and a next-month milk income forecast a Sri Lankan farmer can act on.

The system is decision support. It does not replace a veterinarian, and a severe or contagious result is meant to trigger a call, not to close the case.

04

Research objectives

The main objective is to convert the daily decisions of a traditional cattle farm into a measured, model-backed workflow on a mobile application.

  1. Detect eight visual classes — contagious disease, dermatophilosis, foot-and-mouth disease, healthy, lumpy skin, mastitis, pediculosis, and ringworm — and select the higher-confidence result from DenseNet121 and YOLOv8x.
  2. Grade severity as mild, moderate, or severe, and recommend a treatment protocol from clinical features such as weight, age, and temperature.
  3. Detect cattle behaviour from images and video, and flag patterns that depart from a normal range or from the animal’s baseline.
  4. Record body weight by manual weight measurement, then recommend a daily feed and a nutrition plan from production and health inputs.
  5. Predict whether calving is near, and estimate egg-hatch probability from incubation conditions.
  6. Forecast next month’s milk price change, price per litre, and income in Sri Lankan rupees.
  7. Identify individual cattle and keep detections, feeds, and predictions on a herd record in one application.

05

Methodology

Phase 1Frame

After proposal feedback on 2 May 2026, the study was redirected from a literacy tool to intelligent cattle farming. Objectives were split across disease, feeding, reproduction, and market.

Phase 2Data

Disease images cover eight classes. Behaviour images cover nine behaviours. Tabular sets cover severity, treatment, nutrition, birth, hatch conditions, and historical milk prices.

Phase 3Models

Networks were trained in notebooks, then saved for inference. Disease detection runs both vision models and keeps the higher confidence before the severity and treatment models run.

Phase 4Product

A Flask API serves every model. A Flutter app is the farmer interface. Laravel stores users, cows, and prediction history. Results are checked against accuracy, confidence, and response time.

How a diagnosis is produced

The image is resized and passed through DenseNet121 and, when requested, YOLOv8x. The winning label and its confidence are returned. A manually measured weight, age, temperature, and any previous disease then enter the gradient-boosting severity and treatment models. The same weight measurement is an input to the feed and nutrition models. If a video is sent, frames are sampled and scored for behaviour and disease along a timeline. Abnormal eating, lying, steps, rumination, or temperature can raise an alert against published normal ranges or that cow’s saved baseline.
